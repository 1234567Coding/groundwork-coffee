How to Read a Coffee Bag Label: Roast Date, Process, Altitude

By Groundwork Coffee Team · September 22, 2026 · 8 min read

Coffee bags are covered in words — "washed," "natural," "1,800 MASL," "notes of bergamot" — that mean nothing to a new buyer and just enough to an experienced one to be dangerous. This is a plain-English decoder. Learn it once, and every bag you ever pick up becomes a 30-second read instead of a gamble.

Roast Date vs. "Best By": The Only Date That Matters

There are two dates you might see on a bag, and only one of them means anything:

  • Roast date — the day the beans came out of the roaster. This is the date you want. A roaster proud of freshness prints it; a roaster hiding staleness doesn't.
  • "Best by" date — marketing. It's usually 6–12 months after roasting, which tells you nothing about where the coffee is in its actual life.

The freshness window: coffee peaks roughly 7–21 days off roast for filter brewing (espresso wants a bit more rest, 10–28 days). It's still good through about week 4. Past 5–6 weeks, the sweet, aromatic compounds fade and the cup goes flat and sharp — and no grind adjustment will save it.

Rule of thumb: if the bag has no roast date at all, assume it's old. Good roasters print it because it's a selling point.

Roast Level: What Light, Medium, and Dark Predict

Roast level is the single biggest predictor of what the cup will taste like — bigger than origin, bigger than process:

  • Light roast: bright, acidic, tea-like, most origin character. Dense beans that need hot water (200–205°F) and a fine-ish grind to extract fully. The #1 source of "why is this sour" complaints from new buyers.
  • Medium roast: balanced — sweetness plus some brightness, medium body. The most forgiving roast level and the best starting point if you don't know what you like.
  • Dark roast: chocolatey, smoky, heavy body, low acidity. Extracts easily — use cooler water (185–195°F) or it turns bitter and ashy. Best for French press, moka pot, and milk drinks.

Watch for weasel words: "medium-dark," "full city," "espresso roast." There's no legal standard — one roaster's "medium" is another's "dark." When in doubt, look at the beans through the bag window: oily and near-black is dark no matter what the label claims.

Process: Washed vs. Natural vs. Honey, in One Paragraph Each

Process is how the coffee cherry's fruit was removed from the seed before drying, and it shapes flavor more than most beginners expect:

  • Washed: the fruit is washed off before drying. Clean, bright, crisp cups where you taste the bean itself — florals, citrus, tea-like clarity. The "classic specialty coffee" profile. Most forgiving to brew.
  • Natural: the cherry dries whole, fruit and all. Fruity, winey, heavy-bodied — blueberry, strawberry, boozy sweetness. Can taste wild or funky; naturals divide opinion like nothing else in coffee.
  • Honey: some fruit pulp ("mucilage") is left on during drying — no actual honey involved. The middle path: sweeter and rounder than washed, cleaner than natural. Common in Costa Rican and Colombian coffees.

If you're buying your first specialty bag: washed. It's the clearest window into what good coffee tastes like.

Origin and Altitude: What They Signal (and What's Marketing)

Origin (country, region, farm) tells you the general flavor family: Ethiopian coffees trend floral and citrusy, Colombians trend sweet and balanced, Brazilians trend chocolatey and nutty, Sumatrans trend earthy and heavy. Useful as a rough compass, not a guarantee — a natural-process Brazil can taste fruitier than a washed Ethiopia.

Altitude (listed as MASL — meters above sea level) is the nerdiest line on the bag. The short version: higher altitude generally means denser beans, brighter acidity, and more complex flavor — which is why you'll see 1,800–2,200 MASL bragged about on Ethiopians and Kenyans. Below ~1,200 MASL, expect mellower, less complex cups. It's a signal, not a promise: great coffee grows at 1,400 MASL and mediocre coffee grows at 2,000.

What's mostly marketing: "single origin" (nearly all specialty coffee is single origin — it's table stakes, not a feature), "100% Arabica" (same — virtually all specialty coffee is Arabica), and "shade grown" / "bird friendly" without certification (nice ideas, unverifiable on most bags).

Tasting Notes: How to Read Them Without Believing Them

"Notes of bergamot, apricot, and dark chocolate" is not a list of ingredients — it's the roaster's poetic description of what they tasted. Treat tasting notes as a vibe check, not a promise:

  • Fruit/floral words (apricot, jasmine, blueberry) → expect brightness and lightness. Usually light-roast washed or natural coffees.
  • Sweet words (caramel, honey, brown sugar) → expect a balanced, crowd-pleasing cup. Usually medium roasts.
  • Dark words (dark chocolate, smoke, molasses) → expect heavy body, low acidity. Usually medium-dark to dark roasts.

You will not taste literal blueberries. You might taste "something fruity and sweet that reminds me of blueberries once someone says so." That's normal — palates calibrate with practice, and the notes are a map, not the territory.

The 30-Second Bag Scan: A Worked Example

Here's the whole skill applied to one bag. Imagine you're holding this:

Ethiopia — Guji Zone
Washed process · 2,100 MASL
Light roast · Roasted Sept 10, 2026
Tasting notes: apricot, jasmine, bergamot

Your 30-second read: (1) Roast date is 12 days ago — right in the peak window, buy it. (2) Light roast + washed + high altitude + floral notes = a bright, tea-like, expressive cup. (3) Brew it like a light roast: 200–205°F water, grind on the finer side of medium, 1:16. (4) If it tastes sour, that's under-extraction, not the bean — grind finer before blaming the bag.

Do that scan on every bag and you'll stop buying stale dark roasts when you wanted bright Ethiopians — the most common and most avoidable disappointment in home coffee.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does "single origin" mean better coffee?

Not necessarily — it just means the beans come from one place rather than a blend. Blends are often designed for consistency and espresso; single origins showcase a specific farm or region. Quality depends on the coffee, not the category.

How long after the roast date is coffee still good?

Peak flavor is roughly days 7–21 off roast for filter coffee. It's still enjoyable through week 4, noticeably faded by week 6, and stale beyond that. Whole beans last longer than pre-ground at every stage.

What does MASL stand for?

Meters Above Sea Level — the altitude the coffee was grown at. Higher altitude generally correlates with denser beans and brighter, more complex cups, though it's one signal among many.

Should beginners avoid natural-process coffees?

Not avoid — just know what you're getting into. Naturals can taste funky or winey, which surprises people expecting "normal coffee." Try a washed coffee first to calibrate your palate, then explore naturals when you're curious.
