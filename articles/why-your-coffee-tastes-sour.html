Why Your Coffee Tastes Sour (and the 3 Fixes That Work)

By Groundwork Coffee Team · September 15, 2026 · 8 min read

That sharp, mouth-puckering bite at the front of your sip — like biting into an unripe apple — is the single most common complaint in home coffee, and most articles respond with a list of ten possible causes that leaves you more paralyzed than before. Here's the truth: sour coffee is under-extraction, full stop. Water didn't pull enough flavor compounds out of the grounds. There are exactly three levers that fix it, ranked below by impact. Work them in order.

What Sour Actually Tastes Like (vs. Bright)

First, make sure you're diagnosing the right thing. Sour is sharp, thin, and hollow — it hits the sides of your tongue and vanishes, leaving nothing behind. Think lemon juice or unripe fruit. Bright or acidic (the good kind, common in washed Ethiopians and Kenyans) is lively but has sweetness and body underneath it — think ripe orange or green apple with sugar.

The test: let the cup cool for five minutes. Pleasant brightness stays pleasant or gets sweeter as it cools. Harsh sourness stays harsh. If it's still puckering at lukewarm, it's under-extraction, and the fixes below apply.

Fix #1: Grind Finer

This fixes roughly 70% of sour cups, because grind is the variable most people get wrong and the one with the biggest effect. Finer grounds mean more surface area, which means water extracts flavor faster and more completely.

How much finer: one to two clicks finer on a stepped hand grinder (e.g., from click 18 to click 16 on a Timemore C2), or one full number finer on a Baratza Encore. Visually, you're moving from "kosher salt" toward "fine sand" — still clearly granular, nowhere near powder.

The rule: change nothing else. Same beans, same ratio, same water temperature, same pour. Brew one cup, taste it cooled. Still sour? Go one step finer again. The moment the sourness disappears and you taste sweetness instead, stop — you've found your setting. Write it down.

Fix #2: Raise Your Water Temperature

If grinding finer helped but didn't finish the job — or if you're already at the fine end and the cup is still sharp — your water is probably too cool. This is the light-roast trap: dense, lightly roasted beans need serious heat to extract, and brewing them at 195°F is like trying to steep tea in warm water.

  • Light roasts: 200–205°F (93–96°C). If your kettle was set to 195°F, this one change can transform the cup.
  • Medium roasts: 195–200°F.
  • Dark roasts: stay at 185–195°F — dark beans extract easily, and hotter water pushes them bitter instead.

No temperature control? Boil the kettle and pour 30 seconds off the boil — that lands you right around 203–205°F, perfect for light roasts. Waiting a full 60–90 seconds drops you near 195°F. Pick one timing and keep it consistent.

Fix #3: Extend the Brew

If grind and temperature are dialed and the cup is still edging sour, give water more contact time:

  • Lengthen the bloom to a full 45–60 seconds with 2.5–3x the coffee weight in water. Fresh beans off-gas CO₂ that repels water; a rushed bloom means the rest of the brew starts on dry pockets of grounds.
  • Pour in smaller stages instead of one big dump — three pours of ~100g keeps the slurry hotter and more agitated than a single flood.
  • Strengthen the ratio slightly to 1:15. This doesn't directly fix extraction, but a stronger cup reads less thin and sharp while you dial the rest in.

Target total brew time for a 20g/320g V60: 2:30–3:00. If yours is finishing at 2:00, the water simply isn't spending enough time with the coffee.

The 2-Minute Sour-Coffee Checklist

Run this in order before you change anything. Most sour cups fail at step 1 or 2.

  1. Grind: Is it at least as fine as kosher salt? If it's closer to peppercorns, go finer — that's your fix.
  2. Temperature: Light roast below 200°F? Raise it. (Dark roast already at 205°F+? You may be misdiagnosing — see the bean section below.)
  3. Time: Did the brew finish under 2:15? Extend it via finer grind or staged pours.
  4. Freshness: Were the beans ground more than 15 minutes before brewing, or roasted more than 5–6 weeks ago? Stale coffee brews flat and sharp no matter what you do.

When Sour Is the Bean, Not You

Sometimes your technique is fine and the coffee is just… like that. Very light "Nordic-style" roasts and some washed Kenyans are roasted to showcase intense acidity — if you've worked through all three fixes and the cup is vibrant but still lip-puckering, that's the roast profile, not a flaw.

What to do: brew it at 1:15 to add body, let it cool fully (acidity softens dramatically as temperature drops), or simply buy a medium roast next time. There's no shame in preferring chocolatey Brazil over electric Kenya — the best coffee is the one you actually enjoy drinking.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sour coffee the same as acidic coffee?

No. Acidity is a desirable brightness with sweetness behind it; sourness is harsh, thin, and hollow. If the cup tastes like lemon juice with nothing underneath, that's sour — under-extraction.

Will adding more coffee fix sourness?

Partially — a 1:15 ratio tastes stronger and less sharp — but it masks rather than fixes under-extraction. Grind finer first; adjust ratio second.

Can old beans taste sour?

Yes. Beans more than 5–6 weeks off roast (or ground hours before brewing) lose the sweet compounds first, leaving a sharp, flat cup that no grind adjustment rescues. Check the roast date before touching your grinder.

My sour coffee is also weak. Which fix first?

Sour and weak together almost always means the grind is far too coarse — water is rushing through without extracting. Go two steps finer and keep everything else identical.
