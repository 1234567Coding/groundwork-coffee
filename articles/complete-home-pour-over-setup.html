The Complete Home Pour-Over Setup: Gear, Grind, and Technique

By Groundwork Coffee Team · September 8, 2026 · 11 min read

Your pour-over tastes sour on Tuesday and bitter on Thursday, and you changed nothing. That inconsistency is the reason most home brewers quit pour-over — not the technique, which is simple, but the fact that nobody gave them the actual numbers. This guide fixes that: the gear that matters (and the gear that doesn't), exact ratios and temperatures, and a step-by-step method you can repeat tomorrow morning.

What You Actually Need (and What You Can Skip)

A pour-over setup has four essentials and a pile of nice-to-haves. Buy the essentials first; everything else is a luxury.

The dripper. This is the cone or flat-bed brewer the coffee sits in. The three worth knowing:

  • Hario V60 (conical, ~$10–$25): the most popular dripper in the world for a reason. Its spiral ribs and large single hole give you full control over drawdown speed — which also means it's the least forgiving. Great for bright, sweet cups once your technique is dialed; punishing while you're learning.
  • Chemex (conical with thick filters, ~$45–$60): produces the cleanest cup of the three because its bonded filters trap more oils and fines. The trade-off is a slower drawdown and a higher price per filter. Beautiful on the counter; best for people who hate sediment.
  • Kalita Wave (flat bed, ~$30–$40): three small drain holes and a flat bed make it the most forgiving beginner dripper. Water drains evenly no matter how sloppy your pour is, so cups taste consistent sooner. Slightly less clarity than the V60 at its best, but far better than the V60 at its worst.

The grinder. This is the single most important purchase in all of home coffee — more important than the dripper, the kettle, or the beans. A burr grinder crushes beans between two burrs into uniform particles. A blade grinder smashes them into a mix of boulders and dust, and dust over-extracts (bitter) while boulders under-extract (sour). That mix is exactly the sour-one-day, bitter-the-next problem.

Minimum viable: a decent hand grinder like the 1Zpresso Q2 (~$80) or an entry electric like the Baratza Encore (~$130–$170). If you can only upgrade one thing this year, upgrade the grinder — a $150 grinder with a $10 dripper beats a $10 grinder with a $60 dripper every single time.

The kettle. You need thin, controlled pouring. A gooseneck kettle gives you that; a regular kettle gives you a flood. An electric gooseneck with temperature control (the Fellow Stagg EKG is the famous one at ~$165) is the luxury pick. A stovetop gooseneck like the Hario Buono (~$40–$50) pours just as well — you just boil, wait 30–60 seconds off the boil, and pour. Buy the stovetop version unless you know you'll brew daily for years.

The scale. Pour-over without a scale is guessing, and guessing is the whole problem we're solving. Any kitchen scale with 0.1g precision and a timer works — the popular coffee-specific ones run $15–$50. You do not need a $150 Acaia to make great coffee; you need to know you're pouring 250g of water, not 230g.

What you can skip for now: a fancy stand, a "coffee-specific" thermometer (your kettle's temp setting or a $10 kitchen thermometer is fine), and any dripper beyond your first one. One dripper, learned well, beats three drippers learned badly.

Grind Size: The Variable That Controls Everything

Grind size controls how fast water extracts flavor from coffee. Finer grind = more surface area = faster extraction. Coarser = slower. Your target for pour-over is roughly the texture of kosher salt or raw sugar — coarser than table salt, finer than peppercorns.

Here's the practical rule most guides bury: change only one variable at a time, and change grind first. Brewed a cup and it tastes sour and sharp? Grind finer next time. Bitter and drying? Grind coarser. Keep the ratio, temperature, and pour exactly the same so the grind change is the only explanation.

Dial-in reference points (for a V60; Kalita Wave runs slightly coarser, Chemex slightly coarser still):

  • 20g coffee to 320g water, total brew time 2:30–3:00 → grind was about right
  • Finished in under 2:15 and tastes thin/sour → grind finer
  • Stalled past 3:30 and tastes bitter/muddy → grind coarser

If your grinder has numbered settings, write down the setting for every brew for your first two weeks. "Medium-fine" is meaningless; "setting 14 on my Encore" is data. A notebook or phone note is the cheapest upgrade in coffee.

Water: Temperature and Ratio, With Real Numbers

Ratio. Start at 1:16 — 1 gram of coffee for every 16 grams of water. For a standard single cup: 20g coffee to 320g water (about 11 oz brewed). The full usable range is 1:15 to 1:17:

  • 1:15 — stronger, heavier body, slightly less clarity. Good for darker roasts.
  • 1:16 — the balanced starting point for most light and medium roasts.
  • 1:17 — lighter, more tea-like, more clarity. Good for delicate, floral coffees.

Pick one, brew it three times, then move. Do not change ratio and grind on the same day.

Temperature. Use 195–205°F (90–96°C):

  • Light roasts: 200–205°F. Dense beans need heat to extract fully; under-heated light roast is the #1 cause of sour pour-overs.
  • Medium roasts: 195–200°F. The middle ground.
  • Dark roasts: 185–195°F. Dark beans extract easily and turn bitter fast at high heat.

No thermometer? Boil the kettle, take it off the heat, and wait: ~30 seconds drops you near 205°F, ~60–90 seconds lands near 195°F. It's approximate, but it's consistent, and consistency is the goal.

Water quality, briefly: if your tap water tastes fine, it's fine for coffee. Very hard water mutes acidity; very soft water makes cups taste hollow. A basic carbon filter (like a Brita) is the sweet spot — don't buy into $40-a-month mineral packets until everything else is dialed.

The Method: A Repeatable Pour-Over in 5 Steps

This is written for a V60-02 with 20g coffee and 320g water at 200°F. Scale it to your dripper; the structure doesn't change.

Step 1 — Rinse and heat (1 minute). Put the filter in the dripper, place it on your mug or server, and pour hot water through the filter to rinse out paper taste and preheat everything. Dump the rinse water. Add 20g of freshly ground coffee and gently shake the dripper to level the bed.

Step 2 — Bloom (0:00–0:45). Start your timer. Pour ~50g of water (about 2.5x the coffee weight) in slow spirals to saturate all the grounds. Fresh coffee will bubble and swell as CO₂ escapes — that's the bloom. Wait until 0:45. Skipping the bloom is the most common beginner shortcut and the easiest 10% improvement you'll ever make.

Step 3 — The main pour (0:45–1:30). Pour steadily in slow concentric circles, starting from the center and spiraling outward (avoid pouring directly down the filter walls — water running down the paper bypasses the coffee). Bring the total up to ~200g. Keep the water level roughly consistent rather than dumping it all at once; gentle agitation extracts evenly.

Step 4 — The top-up pour (1:30–2:00). When the water level drops to about halfway, pour the remaining water in the same slow spirals to reach 320g total. Give the dripper one gentle swirl to knock grounds off the walls and flatten the bed — a flat bed at the end is the visual sign of an even brew.

Step 5 — Drawdown and serve (2:00–3:00). Let the water drip through. The bed should finish flat and the last drips should fall around 2:30–3:00. Remove the dripper, give the coffee a swirl, and taste it at three temperatures: hot, warm, and lukewarm. You'll be amazed how much the flavor opens up as it cools — judging a cup only when it's piping hot hides half of what's in it.

Common Mistakes (and the One-Line Fix for Each)

  1. Pouring with a regular kettle. The flood of water agitates the bed unevenly and you can't control the flow. Fix: any gooseneck, even the $40 stovetop one.
  2. Grinding the night before. Ground coffee stales in hours, not days — aromatics escape fast once the bean is broken open. Fix: grind right before brewing, every time.
  3. Eyeballing the water. "About two cups" varies by 40g day to day, which swings your ratio from 1:15 to 1:18 without you knowing. Fix: the scale, every brew, until the motions are muscle memory.
  4. Changing three variables at once. New beans and finer grind and hotter water tells you nothing when the cup changes. Fix: one variable per brew, notes every time.
  5. Using boiling water on dark roasts. 212°F on a dark roast extracts the bitter compounds aggressively. Fix: drop to 190°F for dark roasts and taste the difference.
  6. Judging the cup too hot. Bitterness reads louder at high temperature; sweetness shows up as it cools. Fix: wait 3–4 minutes before your final verdict.

How Much Should a Starter Setup Cost?

Honest tiers, no upsell:

  • Minimum that makes great coffee: ~$70. Plastic V60 ($10) + paper filters ($8) + Hario Buono-style stovetop gooseneck (~$40) + basic 0.1g scale ($15). Assumes you already own any burr grinder — if you don't, the grinder comes first and nothing here matters until it does.
  • The sweet spot: ~$200–$250. Add a Baratza Encore-class grinder ($130–$170) to the above. This setup will make coffee as good as 90% of cafés, and it's where most home brewers should land and stay for a year.
  • Enthusiast: $400+. Temperature-control kettle, premium hand grinder or prosumer electric, multiple drippers. Buy this when you've outgrown the sweet spot and can articulate why — not before.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the best pour-over dripper for beginners?

The Kalita Wave. Its flat bed and three-hole design forgive uneven pouring, so your cups taste consistent while your technique is still developing. Move to a V60 later if you want more control and brighter cups.

Do I really need a burr grinder?

Yes — it's the one non-negotiable. A blade grinder produces uneven particles that brew sour and bitter simultaneously, and no technique fixes that. A hand burr grinder around $80 outperforms any blade grinder ever made.

What coffee-to-water ratio should I start with?

1:16 — for example, 20g of coffee to 320g of water. Brew it at least three times before changing anything, then adjust toward 1:15 for more strength or 1:17 for more clarity.

Why does my pour-over taste sour?

Almost always under-extraction: grind too coarse, water too cool (especially with light roasts), or brew time too short. Grind one step finer and make sure your water is 200–205°F for light roasts.

Is a gooseneck kettle really necessary?

For pour-over, effectively yes. Flow control is half of the technique — a regular kettle dumps water too fast to pour in controlled spirals. A $40 stovetop gooseneck does the job; the $165 electric ones are about convenience, not better pouring.
