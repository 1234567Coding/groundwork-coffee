    Best Burr Grinders Under $100 (2026): Tested and Ranked

    By Groundwork Coffee Team · September 12, 2026 · 10 min read

    The grinder is the highest-ROI purchase in home coffee — one good burr grinder fixes more bad cups than every other upgrade combined. But under $100 is a minefield: fake "burr" grinders with serrated discs, rebadged junk with five-star review farms, and blade grinders wearing burr costumes. We bought the contenders, brewed with all of them for two weeks, and found three actually worth owning.

    How We Tested

    Every grinder went through the same protocol: the same bag of medium-roast washed Colombian beans (roasted 10 days prior), ground at pour-over and French press settings, brewed as V60 at 1:16 and 200°F. We scored grind uniformity (sifted through a 400-micron sieve to check fines), retention (how much ground coffee stays trapped inside — weighed in vs. out), noise, grind speed for 20g, and build feel. Taste panels were blind: three cups, no labels, ranked for clarity and sweetness.

    What "Burr" Has to Mean at This Price (and the Fakes to Avoid)

    A real burr set is two machined steel (or ceramic) discs with cutting teeth that shear beans into even particles. Below $100, real burrs exist — the hand grinders here all have them. What to avoid:

    • No-name "conical burr" electrics under $40. Open one up and you'll find stamped, serrated discs that crush inconsistently. They produce the boulder-and-dust mix that brews sour and bitter in the same cup — the exact problem you're trying to escape.
    • Anything that calls itself a burr grinder but has one speed and one button. Real burr grinders need adjustable burr distance. No adjustment = no dial-in.
    • Blade grinders, full stop. They're $20 for a reason, and the reason is uneven particle size no technique can fix.

    Best Overall: Timemore Chestnut C2 (~$69)

    The C2 shouldn't be this good for the money. Its 38mm stainless steel burrs produced the most uniform grind in our under-$100 testing — fines measured under 8% by weight at pour-over settings, versus 14–18% for the budget electrics. Twenty grams takes about 40 seconds of easy cranking, retention is under 0.3g, and the all-metal body feels like it costs twice the price.

    Grind range covers pour-over, Aeropress, French press, and cold brew comfortably. It can technically grind fine enough for a moka pot, though we wouldn't push it to true espresso. The stepped adjustment (36 clicks) is coarse enough that dialing in espresso is fiddly — but at this price, nobody's buying it for espresso anyway.

    Buy it if: you brew 1–2 cups a day and don't mind 40 seconds of hand grinding. Skip it if: you have wrist issues or brew for a crowd — hand grinding 60g for a Chemex gets old fast.

    Best Electric Under $100: Capresso Infinity Plus (~$99)

    If hand grinding is a non-starter, this is the electric to buy. Sixteen grind settings on a real conical burr set, a geared motor that runs slow enough not to cook the beans, and a timer dial so you can dose repeatably. In our tests it matched the C2 on flavor clarity for pour-over — the difference showed up in the sieve, with a few more fines, and in cups that tasted very slightly muddier at French press coarseness.

    Limits are honest: it's loud (about as loud as a cheap blender for 15 seconds), the plastic hopper builds static that flings grounds around the counter, and the finest settings still won't do real espresso. But for drip, pour-over, and French press, it's the best electric value we've tested under $100.

    Buy it if: you want push-button grinding for daily brewing. Skip it if: you plan to get into espresso within a year — you'll outgrow it.

    Best Hand Grinder for Small Kitchens and Travel: 1Zpresso Q2 (~$79)

    The Q2 is the slim travel pick: it fits inside an Aeropress plunger for packing, which is genuinely clever. Ninety clicks of adjustment with roughly 25 microns per click gives it the finest, most precise adjustment of anything here — enough to actually dial in a moka pot or a manual espresso brewer like a Flair, which neither the C2 nor the Capresso can do well.

    The trade-off is capacity (about 20g — one cup) and speed: the smaller 38mm burr set takes closer to a minute for 20g at pour-over settings. For one person brewing one cup, that's a non-issue. For a household, it's a bottleneck.

    Buy it if: you travel with coffee gear or want the most precise adjustment under $100. Skip it if: you brew more than two cups at a time.

    What You Give Up vs. $150+ Grinders

    Honest trade-offs, because the $130–$170 tier (Baratza Encore and its peers) exists for a reason:

    • Speed and capacity. The Encore grinds 20g in under 10 seconds and holds 225g of beans. Hand grinders can't touch that workflow.
    • Espresso range. None of these three truly do espresso. The Q2 gets closest, but a dedicated espresso grinder starts around $200 for a reason.
    • Noise and static. Budget electrics are loud and messy. The C2 sidesteps this entirely by being manual — one of its quiet advantages.
    • Longevity of parts. Baratza sells replacement burrs and parts for a decade; budget brands mostly don't. Expect 2–4 years of daily use from the picks here, versus 5–10 from the next tier up.

    Worth the stretch to $150? If you brew every single day and hate hand grinding, yes — the Baratza Encore (~$130–$170) is the electric we'd buy with our own money at the next tier. Better parts support, faster, and a meaningful step up in grind uniformity. If you brew a few times a week, the C2 at $69 gets you 90% of the cup quality for half the price.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Hand grinder or electric under $100?

    Hand, unless you physically can't or won't crank. At this price, hand grinders have better burrs and more uniform grinds than electrics — the Timemore C2 out-ground every electric under $100 we tested. Electrics win on convenience only.

    How long do cheap grinders last?

    Two to four years of daily use is realistic for the picks here. Steel burrs themselves last 300–500 pounds of coffee; what fails first is usually plastic housings, switches, or motors on the electrics. Hand grinders tend to outlast electrics at this price because there's less to break.

    Can a sub-$100 grinder do espresso?

    Not real espresso. Espresso needs very fine, very consistent grinds with tiny adjustment steps, and that engineering starts around $200 (hand) to $300+ (electric). The 1Zpresso Q2 can handle moka pot and manual lever brewers, which is close enough for many people — but don't buy any of these three expecting café shots from a pump machine.
